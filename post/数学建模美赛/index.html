MCM/ICM Retrospective: Problem Selection, Clear Reasoning, and Visual Communication

Lessons from our 2020 Problem A Finalist submission, including a costly change of direction and the value of a coherent technical narrative.

Lynn LeePublished 2020-05-035 min read
Read in Chinese →

An archived study note, kept with its original date. This English reading edition clarifies version-sensitive statements; the original Chinese is available via the language switch.

Our 2020 MCM submission for Problem A received a Finalist award. I was pleased that the work was recognized, while also wishing we had taken it further. The experience differed from the national contest in several ways, especially problem selection and interpreting an open-ended brief. These notes focus on the decisions and working habits behind the submission rather than presenting our solution as a model answer.

Our change of direction

The 2020 event took place under unusual pandemic conditions, with two competition periods. We initially hoped to wait until March so that the team could return to campus and work together. When the first group of problems became available, we decided to use them as practice and potentially submit if the work went well.

That group contained Problems A, D, and E. As we understood them at the time, A involved a continuous model in a fishing context, D involved planning, and E involved evaluating a plastics-related issue. We spent much of the first morning reading and discussing the questions, then chose E. Evaluation problems felt familiar; we were less confident about continuous physical models, and D was difficult for us to interpret.

Working on E exposed a problem that subject familiarity had hidden: we could not agree on the interpretation of the task, and searching for supporting material remained difficult. After losing more than half a day, we decided to stop and switch to A.

A’s brief was clearer to us. Although its continuous-model component was outside our strongest area, several of its forecasting and evaluation steps were familiar. The original post linked to a Chinese translation of Problem A . The switch was uncomfortable and left little spare time, but it was better than continuing without a shared understanding of the problem.

A more deliberate selection process

Understand the kinds of questions

In the problem structure discussed in these 2020 notes, MCM covered continuous, discrete, and data-oriented problems, while ICM covered operations research or networks, environmental topics, and policy. These labels helped organize practice, but they were not enough to determine fit. A familiar category could still contain a brief the team interpreted inconsistently.

I treated the data-oriented category as data science rather than assuming that the term “big data” implied a particular dataset size. Likewise, an environmental or policy question still needed a defensible model and clear reasoning, not just a broad discussion.

Read independently before discussing

A useful sequence is:

  1. Each teammate reads the brief independently, translates unfamiliar wording, and sketches the main modeling tasks.
  2. Compare interpretations and eliminate problems the team is clearly unable to tackle.
  3. For the remaining candidates, inspect relevant literature, available data, and possible approaches.
  4. Use prior practice to judge whether the team can complete a coherent solution in time.

Independent reading makes disagreements visible. If everyone follows the first person’s interpretation, the team can become confidently wrong together. Looking for references also helps reveal whether an apparent solution path is grounded in usable evidence.

I tended to prefer problems with enough reliable material to support the work. That was a personal preference for managing uncertainty, not a claim that the most documented problem is always the best choice. A team with different strengths may reasonably choose a more open problem.

Let rehearsals inform the decision

For us, the central tradeoff was clear: A had an unfamiliar component but a shared interpretation; E used familiar methods but an uncertain brief. Practice across several categories helped us recognize that distinction.

Before MCM/ICM, we practiced five of the six problem categories. Broader practice would have been useful, particularly complete rehearsals beginning with selection rather than starting with a preselected problem. Knowing the team’s strengths and weak points gives a concrete basis for decisions when no option feels easy.

Make the solution’s reasoning visible

Many modeling problems are simplified versions of real research or engineering questions. Literature can supply starting assumptions, methods, and evidence, but the final paper still has to explain how those pieces fit this particular problem.

During preparation, we read many strong national-contest and MCM papers. The quality that stood out most consistently was a clear line of reasoning with appropriate explanation. Sophisticated algorithms appeared in some submissions, but complexity alone was not what made the work convincing.

One 2019 Problem A paper we studied used relatively simple models and bold simplifications. Its argument remained coherent, its assumptions were understandable, and its figures supported the explanation. Because we had worked through that problem ourselves, we could appreciate how well the paper communicated its choices. The lesson was not that simplification guarantees success; it was that a defensible, clearly explained model can be stronger than a complicated model with an opaque justification.

The weaker papers I encountered often named advanced methods without explaining why they were appropriate. The overall argument could still contain gaps or contradictions. I would rather show the reader a sound chain from assumptions to results than fill the paper with techniques whose role is unclear.

Visual communication is part of the reasoning

Well-designed figures and clean layout make a paper easier to assess. This was an observation from reading submissions, not an official judging criterion or a promise of an award. A diagram is valuable when it makes a relationship, process, or spatial pattern easier to understand than prose or a table.

The most useful lessons from this submission were choosing a problem the team could interpret consistently, changing direction when that premise failed, and making each modeling decision understandable to someone outside the team.
