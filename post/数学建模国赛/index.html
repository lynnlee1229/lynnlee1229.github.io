Lessons from China's Undergraduate Mathematical Modeling Contest

A retrospective on team preparation, modeling practice, implementation, and communicating a solution under a three-day deadline.

Lynn LeePublished 2020-02-226 min read
Read in Chinese →

An archived study note, kept with its original date. This English reading edition clarifies version-sensitive statements; the original Chinese is available via the language switch.

I wrote these notes as an undergraduate after participating in the 2019 China Undergraduate Mathematical Contest in Modeling (CUMCM), where our team received a National First Prize. I also entered the 2020 MCM/ICM; a later update to the original post recorded our Finalist result.

After more than a year of modeling competitions, preparing for and submitting the MCM paper felt like the end of that chapter. Other participants’ advice had helped us along the way, so I wanted to document what worked for our team. This first retrospective focuses on CUMCM and undergraduate participation.

The competition in context

CUMCM asks a small team to turn a practical problem into a mathematical model, solve it, and explain the result in a paper. In the 2019 format discussed here, three teammates had roughly three days to complete the work. The challenge combined modeling, implementation, literature and data research, visualization, and technical writing.

The educational value was broader than learning individual algorithms. It required us to find useful information quickly, understand unfamiliar concepts, make assumptions explicit, collaborate under pressure, and express a solution clearly.

The original post summarized the 2019 rules and paper format using the official CUMCM website and background material found through Baidu . Those details are historical, not a statement of current contest requirements. Our submission included a summary, the main argument, and supporting code and material. The code had to be runnable and consistent with the results reported in the paper. The practical lesson was to plan for the complete deliverable, not just a working model.

Build a team that can work together

Cover the main responsibilities

The three main responsibilities were building the model, implementing and solving it, and writing the paper. A good team needs coverage of all three, but academic majors do not have to map rigidly to roles. Our team assigned work according to strengths: the teammate with the strongest modeling ideas focused on the model, the strongest writer handled exposition and layout, and the strongest programmer focused on implementation.

The roles should overlap. A useful arrangement is a modeler who can also program, a programmer who can also reason about the model, and a writer who takes responsibility for figures and diagrams. A physics-oriented problem may need more modeling effort, while a data-analysis problem may need more implementation. Some flexibility prevents one person from becoming the bottleneck.

Align schedules and expectations

The contest itself was short, but preparation was not. We began more than a month before the summer break, paused for final exams, spent roughly a month practicing during the summer, and organized another intensive period before the contest.

Overlapping availability matters more than an ambitious plan that nobody can follow. So does a shared level of commitment. Agree on the goal and the time each person can invest before committing to a team.

Complementary working styles also help. An imaginative teammate can propose alternatives; a careful teammate can challenge assumptions and catch inconsistencies. The decisive factor is how well the team learns to cooperate, not whether its initial profile looks ideal on paper.

Practice the complete workflow

Establish the fundamentals

Calculus, linear algebra, and probability provide the mathematical foundation. Modeling adds the habit of translating a real situation into variables, assumptions, constraints, and an answerable question. A systematic introduction to common models is useful; our preparation included books and online lectures, such as Si Shoukui’s book on mathematical modeling algorithms and applications.

I think of a basic model as the structure of the solution. Refinements and more sophisticated algorithms should improve that structure rather than disguise a weak starting point.

Rehearse with past problems

We practiced by reserving a continuous three-day period and working through a past contest from problem selection to a complete paper. Our team completed seven sets of problems. That number is context, not a target every team needs to match.

The value came from three things:

  1. A shared deadline made preparation concrete and helped us keep each other accountable.
  2. Finishing a problem exposed gaps that reading alone did not reveal.
  3. Comparing our work with strong submissions showed what we had overlooked.

Each role should extract different lessons from the same rehearsal. The modeler can study assumptions, validation, and alternative formulations. In our first data-analysis practice, for example, we did not give model validation enough attention. The writer can study argument structure, captions, diagrams, and the choice between a chart and a table. The programmer can retain tested, understandable implementations for later reuse.

Build a code library you understand

Save and organize programs from practice. If examples or libraries come from elsewhere, get them running and understand their assumptions, inputs, and adjustable components before relying on them. A contest deadline is a poor time to discover that an algorithm’s implementation does not match your problem.

MATLAB and Python both supported our scientific-computing needs. My advice at the time was to deepen whichever one a participant already knew; starting from scratch, I found MATLAB’s integrated environment and documentation approachable for contest work. The point was operational fluency, not declaring one language universally superior.

I also learned that visual quality depends on deliberate choices rather than the language alone. MATLAB plots can improve substantially with suitable styling and layout.

Treat the paper and working conditions as part of the project

Make the argument easy to follow

The strong papers we studied were readable and carefully laid out. That observation does not establish an award rule, but it reinforced the importance of presentation. We invested time in figures and learned LaTeX during practice so that formatting would consume less attention during the contest.

The goal is to explain the solution: which assumptions matter, why a model was selected, what the results mean, and where its limits are. Attractive figures help when they make that reasoning easier to inspect.

Reduce avoidable friction

Our university did not provide the arrangement we wanted, so we chose a shared hotel workspace for the contest. The general lesson was to arrange a stable place to work together and avoid repeated moves that consumed time and interrupted concentration.

Rest mattered more than we initially expected. I stayed up all night on the first day and found it difficult to program effectively the next day. A short period of sleep helped me work again. That experience made the cost of exhaustion concrete: important modeling and coding decisions need a clear head.

Support the team when progress stalls

On the second day, both modeling and implementation were progressing slowly, and morale suffered. We encouraged one another and took short breaks in different ways. Looking back, that mutual support was essential to finishing. A technically strong team can still fail if it cannot recover from frustration together.

These are observations from one team’s preparation and contest experience, not a universal formula for winning. The lasting habits were practicing end to end, sharing enough context across roles, keeping runnable evidence, and writing so that another person can follow the reasoning. I remain grateful to my teammates for the work we did together.
